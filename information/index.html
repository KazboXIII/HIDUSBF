INFORMATION / CONTROLLERS

Know what you’re
actually buying.

Hall effect. TMR. 8K. Five-pin. Controller listings are full of labels. This page explains each one in plain English, and keeps what has been measured apart from what is only claimed.

Research checked 30 October 2026About a 10 minute read, or 1 minute if you only read the bold lines

THE SHORT VERSION

  1. Read the full model name. “Pro”, “8K PC”, “2C” or “Wired TMR” can mean different sticks, rates and consoles.
  2. Hall and TMR sticks resist drift. They don’t make it impossible.
  3. 8,000 Hz is how often updates are sent. It is not your input lag.
  4. A number on the box is a claim until someone measures it on the same model, firmware and connection.
  5. Custom boards are a different controller in a familiar shell. Check what still works, and where.

01 / JARGON IN ONE LINE

The words you’ll see, explained.

Skim this once and the rest of the page (and most product listings) gets much easier.

Polling rate (Hz)
How often the PC asks the controller for an update. 1,000 Hz is once every millisecond.
Report rate
How many updates actually arrive each second. It can be lower than the polling rate.
Input lag (latency)
Time from your press to the result. It needs its own test; you can’t work it out from Hz.
Stick drift
The stick reports movement when nobody is touching it.
Potentiometer stick
The classic stick (Alps is the well-known maker). A contact slides over a track, and that track can wear.
Hall effect
A magnetic stick sensor. Nothing slides inside the sensor itself.
TMR
Tunnelling magnetoresistance: a different magnetic sensor. Also contactless, and not a kind of Hall.
JS13, RJ13, FJH10K…
Model names of stick modules, not sensor types. Always check the full part name.
3-pin / 5-pin
How a stick is wired. It tells you about fit, not speed.
Mainboard
The controller’s main electronics. Custom builds may swap it for a different board, such as Marius.
Remap kit
Adds back paddles that press existing buttons. The original board stays.
Deadzone
A zone (around centre, or at the edge) where stick movement is ignored or capped.
Calibration
Telling the controller where centre and the edges are. It fixes offsets, not worn parts.
ADC / bits
Turns stick position into a number. More bits means more possible values, not automatically more accuracy.
XInput
The “Xbox controller” interface most Windows games read. Other controllers can pretend to be one.

02 / STICK SENSORS

Three ways to read a stick.

The sensor measures where the stick is. It doesn’t set the polling rate, the stick tension or which consoles the controller works with.

CONTACT

Potentiometer

A contact slides over a resistive track and the voltage tells the board where the stick is.

For you: standard in many stock controllers. A worn or dirty track is a classic cause of drift.

MAGNETIC

Hall effect

A sensor reads the field of a magnet that moves with the stick.

For you: no sliding contact in the sensor, so that wear point is gone. Springs and gimbals still wear.

MAGNETIC

TMR

A magnetic element whose resistance changes with the field. Different physics from Hall.

For you: also contactless. Often sold as more sensitive or lower power; judge the finished controller, not the sensor name.

How they work: Alps Alpine, Texas Instruments, TDK.

So is “anti-drift” true?

Partly. Hall and TMR remove the most common wear point. A magnetic stick can still drift from a tired spring, loose parts, a misaligned magnet, poor calibration or nearby magnets. One stick maker warns that Xbox trigger magnets can affect its TMR sticks. Read “anti-drift” as “drift-resistant”. Error sources ↗ AKNES V7 warning ↗

Magnetic doesn’t mean digital. Hall and TMR sticks can send an analog signal, just like a potentiometer, for the board to convert. Others use a digital interface. TI design examples ↗

03 / POLLING RATE & LAG

Hz is how often. Not how fast.

Polling rate is how often your PC checks the controller. A higher rate shortens the wait between updates. It doesn’t tell you how long the controller takes to notice your press, or how long the game and screen take to show it.

WHAT 8K DOES

More frequent updates

Up to 8,000 reports a second, one every 0.125 ms, so the game is never waiting long for the next one.

WHAT 8K DOESN’T PROMISE

0.125 ms input lag

Or fresh stick data in every report, or any change over Bluetooth or on a console. Sensor reading, processing, the game and your display all add time.

Words to tell apart

Polling rate is what the PC asks for. Report rate is what actually arrives. Sampling rate is how often the controller reads its own sticks and buttons. Latency is press-to-result time. A claim should say which one it means. USB interval docs ↗ DeepPoll’s scope ↗

Why a tester might show about 1,000 Hz

Some testers count how often a stick value changes, not how many reports arrive. A report can carry the same stick values as the one before it, so these tools can read lower than the real report rate. Use a tool that counts reports for polling, and a proper latency test for lag. Polling tester source ↗

Browser gamepad testers usually update in step with your screen’s refresh. They’re great for checking behaviour, not for measuring USB rate. Browser timing ↗

What HIDUSBF can and can’t change

HIDUSBF changes how often Windows polls a USB controller. It can’t change the sensor, add precision the hardware never captured, or make the controller’s firmware read its sticks faster. Asking for 8K doesn’t prove the controller sends 8K, so check the measured rate. It does nothing for Bluetooth or consoles. Original driver docs ↗

One measured example Measured

On the developer’s PC, a DualSense Edge on USB with HIDUSBF 4.0.2 delivered 80,009 reports in 10.0 seconds, about 7,999 a second, counted as they were read. That shows 8K report delivery on that setup. It doesn’t show 8K fresh stick readings, lower input lag, or what another controller or PC will do.

Bits aren’t the same as precision

8 bits can hold 256 values, 12 bits 4,096, 16 bits 65,536. That’s how many values can be stored. Noise, sensor quality and filtering decide how many are useful. XInput always uses 16-bit stick fields, whatever the stick underneath could measure. XINPUT_GAMEPAD ↗

04 / MEASURED VS CLAIMED

A decoder for spec sheets.

Most numbers in a listing are one of four kinds. Knowing which is half the battle.

Measured Someone tested a named unit, with a stated method.

Maker spec What the manufacturer says. Useful, but not independent.

Arithmetic Worked out from another number, not tested.

Marketing A phrase with no test attached.

“8,000 Hz polling”

Maker spec

Usually true only in certain modes. Check wired vs 2.4 GHz vs Bluetooth, PC vs console, and whether anyone measured the report rate.

“0.125 ms input lag”

Arithmetic

It’s just 1,000 ÷ 8,000, the gap between reports. Real lag needs a test that names its method.

“Anti-drift” / “never drifts”

Marketing

Magnetic sticks remove one cause of drift. Check the warranty and whether replacement sticks are sold. Why →

“12-bit ADC”, “4,000-level positioning”

Maker spec

Describes resolution on paper. Smooth small movements and a steady centre matter more; test those.

“16-bit stick output”

Format

The size of the box, not what’s in it. Filling a bigger field can’t create detail that was never measured.

“0% circularity error”

Measured Narrow

Real, but it only checks the outer edge, and firmware can reshape that. It says nothing about centre, small movements or lag.

“World’s fastest controller”

Marketing

Ignore it unless a test method, controller, firmware and connection are published.

“PS5 shell” / “DS4 mode”

Not a guarantee

Describes the shape or what it pretends to be. Console games can need authentication. Ask about your exact console and game. GP2040-CE notes ↗

“5-pin = better / TMR”

Not a spec

Pin count is about wiring. It doesn’t tell you the sensor type, speed or what it fits. Pins →

Even official pages can disagree with themselves.

Flydigi’s Apex 5 store page gives stick tension as 30–100 gf in one place and 40–100 gf in another. GameSir’s Tarantula 8K PC product page headlines 0.25 ms latency, while its launch post said 0.5 ms response. When a maker’s own numbers conflict, ask which is current; don’t pick the nicer one. Flydigi ↗ GameSir page ↗ launch post ↗

05 / SAME NAME, DIFFERENT CONTROLLER

The suffix is the spec.

These real examples come from the makers’ own pages, checked 30 October 2026. They show how much can change between models with nearly the same name. All rates here are Maker spec.

Razer Wolverine V3 Pro vs V3 Pro 8K PC

V3 Pro: Hall sticks, Xbox and PC. 1,000 Hz only in wired PC Tournament Mode; 250 Hz wireless and on Xbox.

V3 Pro 8K PC: TMR sticks, PC only. 8,000 Hz advertised wired and on its HyperSpeed wireless dongle.

GameSir G7 Pro vs G7 Pro 8K PC

G7 Pro: TMR sticks, works wired on Xbox. 1,000 Hz on PC, wired and 2.4 GHz.

G7 Pro 8K PC: a PC product. Up to 8,000 Hz over USB and 2.4 GHz.

8BitDo Ultimate 2 vs Ultimate 2C

Ultimate 2 Wireless: TMR sticks. 1,000 Hz over USB and 2.4 GHz. Its “8Speed” branding does not mean 8K.

Ultimate 2C Wireless: Hall sticks and Hall triggers; a separate product.

Ultimate 2 ↗ Ultimate 2C ↗

SCUF Valor: Hall or TMR

Wired Hall, wired TMR and wireless TMR versions all exist, and the manual gives different calibration steps for Hall and TMR models.

Valor Pro Wireless’s 1,000 Hz mode is wired PC only.

Manual ↗ Wired TMR ↗ Pro Wireless ↗

Same shell, different inside

A PlayStation-shaped controller can hold:

  • A stock board: exactly as it left the factory.
  • A modified stock board: the original electronics plus paddles, new sticks or trigger stops.
  • A replacement board: different electronics entirely. Features and console support follow the new board, not the badge on the shell.

“Modular” means replaceable, not magnetic. The DualSense Edge’s stick modules swap out by hand, but that says nothing about the sensor inside a replacement. Sony’s instructions ↗

06 / STICK MODULES & PINS

JS13 is a model. TMR is a technology.

A stick module is the whole part: mechanism, sensor, size and wiring. The name on a listing often tells you only one of those.

Frame and sensor can come from different makers. “ALPS base” can mean an Alps frame carrying someone else’s magnetic sensors. Ask who made each.

Tension is a spring, not a setting. Stiffer springs or taller sticks change feel. A sensitivity curve in software changes the output. One doesn’t replace the other.

Five pins aren’t a speed rating

“3-pin” and “5-pin” count connections, and not even always the same ones (one sensor, a connector, or the whole mechanism). Three pins doesn’t mean potentiometer. Five pins doesn’t mean TMR, faster, or universal fit.

Real example: Marius MIDAS.

Battle Beaver documents MIDAS as a five-pin Hall system. In its JDM-5X builds, three-pin and five-pin boards and sticks don’t mix; switching needs the matching board. That rule is for that product, not every five-pin part. Builder docs ↗

Before any swap, confirm: footprint, what each pin does, voltage, output range, axis direction, firmware support and calibration. Fitting the holes is only the first check.

07 / CUSTOM BOARDS

The board matters. So does its version.

Four different jobs get called “a board”:

  • Mainboard: runs the whole controller.
  • Remap board: adds paddles that press existing buttons.
  • Calibration board: adjusts a compatible stick circuit.
  • Windows USB filter (like HIDUSBF): software on the PC, not hardware in the controller.

Ask for the exact board, revision and firmware. Specs from one generation don’t carry over to the next, or back to the last.

Marius / MH family

Maker + builder docs

A separate replacement board, not a Sony board with an overclock. The October 2025 MH4 Analog was announced as PC-only, DS4-format, sold without sticks, and without rumble, sound or gyro. Battle Beaver lists DS4-, DualSense- and Xbox-format Marius builds with native 8K on PC; the shell shape doesn’t mean it works on that console. Announcement ↗ Builder ↗

Its setup mode runs at 100–300 Hz, so don’t measure in setup mode. DeepPoll measures polling, not full input lag. Developer docs ↗ DeepPoll ↗

FireBird / A*

Version-specific docs

The creator’s older manual covers PCB v2.4, tool v2.15 and firmware v2.24, and documents 1,000 Hz outside Switch mode. That isn’t a spec for every DS4 or A* build. The newer browser configurator warns that downgrading can lose settings. Match board, firmware, mode and tool before changing anything. Manual ↗ Configurator ↗

Helium Strike / HyperStrike (HS2)

Couldn’t fully verify

These names appear in builder listings, but we couldn’t confirm a complete hardware spec and revision list. So we don’t give them a polling rate, console list or feature list. Ask the builder for the exact board, firmware and supported modes. HS2 configurator ↗

SuiOvOi

Configurator

Its configurator has separate settings for USB report rate, stick sampling rate and precision, a good illustration that these are different numbers. Settings in a menu aren’t measurements. Configurator ↗

GP2040-CE

Open project docs

Open firmware for RP2040 boards, popular in arcade and leverless controllers. Not a drop-in DualSense board. Its docs are clear that “PS5 mode” has authentication and game-type limits. Compatibility docs ↗

Remap kits (eXtremeRate and similar)

Exact-SKU docs

Add rear buttons to the original board. Compatibility is per kit and per controller revision: for example, eXtremeRate’s RISE4 Plus MAX (HKCPFJ001M3) lists specific DualSense board revisions. Kit spec ↗

8K doesn’t automatically cost you features, but a new board might.

Audio, rumble, gyro, touch tracking, adaptive triggers, battery and wireless depend on the board and build. A touchpad can still click while touch tracking is gone. Ask for a list of what’s kept.

08 / CALIBRATION & TESTING

A clean circle isn’t the whole test.

Centre calibration
Sets where “neutral” is. Fixes an offset; can’t fix a loose or damaged stick.
Range calibration
Sets the edges. Afterwards, check every direction and diagonal, not just left and right.
Inner deadzone
Ignores small movement near centre. Hides noise and offsets, but leaves a spot with no response. Zero isn’t always best.
Outer deadzone
Where full output kicks in. Set too aggressively, it clips your travel.
Response curve
How stick movement maps to output. Personal preference and game-dependent, not a sensor feature.
Snapback filter
Hides the brief opposite input when a released stick overshoots centre. Effect depends on the setting.

Concepts from the FireBird manual and Microsoft’s deadzone examples.

After replacing a stick, follow that part’s own steps. GuliKit, for example, says to close the case and fit the screws before calibrating. A generic Windows or browser calibration may not write the right settings to your controller, and a replacement board that imitates a DualShock may not accept Sony calibration at all. GuliKit ↗ dualshock-tools ↗

A two-minute check

  1. Leave both sticks alone. Look for offset or jitter.
  2. Press the triggers without touching the sticks. The sticks shouldn’t move.
  3. Make tiny movements. Look for dead spots or jumps.
  4. Sweep slowly through the full range, diagonals included.
  5. Let go from different directions. Check it returns to centre without bouncing past.
  6. Repeat with your real in-game deadzone, on the connection you actually use.

This checks behaviour. It doesn’t measure input lag or predict how long the sticks will last.

09 / BEFORE YOU BUY

Five questions to answer first.

A good listing answers these without you guessing from a shell photo or a headline number.

  1. What exactly is inside? Board and revision, firmware, full stick model, sensor type and wiring.
  2. Where does it work? Your console or PC, wired or wireless, and whether it needs an adapter. Any games that won’t work?
  3. What’s kept and what’s gone? Audio, gyro, rumble, adaptive triggers, touchpad tracking, battery, wireless.
  4. What was measured, and how? Polling or latency? Buttons or sticks? Which firmware and connection?
  5. How is it looked after? Which calibration tool, how to update or recover it, spare parts, and the written warranty.

COPY THIS TO A SELLER

Hi, before I order, could you confirm: (1) the exact board/PCB and firmware version, (2) the stick module part name and sensor type, (3) which consoles and PC modes it works in, wired and wireless, (4) which features are kept (rumble, gyro, audio, touchpad, adaptive triggers, wireless), (5) whether the polling rate or latency was measured and how, and (6) how calibration and repairs are handled? Thanks!

Red flags

  • Only a photo of the shell; no board or stick named.
  • A latency figure with no method, firmware or connection.
  • “Universal fit”, “never drifts” or “works on PS5” with no limits listed.
  • A rate quoted without saying wired, 2.4 GHz or Bluetooth.

The right controller is the one whose fit, feel and measured behaviour suit how you play. A newer sensor can be an upgrade without making your current controller obsolete.
