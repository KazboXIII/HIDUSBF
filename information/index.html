01 / THE HARDWARE LAYERS

Same shell. Different controller.

A PlayStation-shaped controller can contain the original Sony mainboard, an original board with modifications, or an entirely different replacement board. Those are different products, even when the outside looks identical.

01

Mechanism

The gimbal, spring, stick height, travel and click switch. These determine much of the physical feel and return to center.

02

Sensor & interface

How movement is measured, and how the signal reaches the board. A model name, a sensing principle and a connector are different descriptions.

03

Mainboard & firmware

The electronics and onboard software that read inputs, apply calibration and filtering, and construct reports.

04

Connection, host & game

USB, Bluetooth or a wireless receiver; the protocol and platform; then the game’s deadzones, response curve and frame timing.

Stock, modified and modular mean different things

A stock controller retains its factory configuration. An OEM-board custom build adds things such as rear buttons, replacement sticks or trigger switches around the original mainboard. A replacement-board build changes the controller’s electronic platform. Compatibility must follow the installed board, not the shell badge.

Modular describes replaceability. Sony’s DualSense Edge has user-replaceable stick assemblies; that feature alone says nothing about the sensing technology inside a replacement. Swapping the complete Edge assembly is also different from desoldering the mechanism inside it. Sony’s replacement instructions ↗

Likewise, XInput or a “DS4 mode” names an input interface or emulation mode. It does not certify every original feature or console authentication requirement. Microsoft’s interface documentation ↗ GP2040-CE compatibility notes ↗

02 / POSITION SENSING

Three ways to read a stick.

The sensing element measures position. It does not, by itself, decide the controller’s polling rate, physical tension or console support.

CONTACT

Potentiometer

A moving contact travels over a resistive element. The changing voltage represents position. Wear or contamination can affect the reading.

Common reference: Alps Alpine RKJXV.

MAGNETIC

Hall effect

A Hall sensor reads a magnetic field as the mechanism moves a magnet. The position sensing does not need a sliding electrical contact.

Contactless sensing; mechanical parts remain.

MAGNETIC

TMR

Tunneling magnetoresistance uses a magnetic element whose electrical resistance changes with the field. It is a different physical effect from Hall sensing.

Also contactless; not a type of Hall effect.

Principles: Alps Alpine, Texas Instruments and TDK.

“Anti-drift” needs a little translation

Hall and TMR eliminate the sliding sensor contact as a wear mechanism. They do not eliminate spring fatigue, loose mechanical parts, magnet alignment errors, electronics faults or poor calibration. A contactless sensor can still report a position away from center. Magnetic joystick design and error sources ↗

TMR can offer useful sensitivity and power advantages in a particular design. That does not establish that every TMR controller is more accurate, faster or more efficient than every Hall controller. Compare the finished implementation and measurements.

Analog vs digital is a separate question.

Hall and TMR sensors can feed analog outputs into a board’s analog-to-digital converter (ADC). Other designs use a digital interface. “Magnetic” does not automatically mean “digital.” TI’s analog-output design examples ↗

03 / MODULE NAMES

JS13 is a model. TMR is a technology.

A module includes more than its sensing principle. Its dimensions, mechanics, output range and electrical arrangement still have to suit the controller.

Frame, sensor and seller may be three different names

A builder can retain an OEM mechanical frame and fit another supplier’s sensors. “ALPS base” therefore need not mean standard ALPS potentiometers remain installed. Ask who made the mechanism and who made the sensors, especially for a hybrid or branded upgrade.

Tension is mechanical resistance. More spring force or a different stick height changes feel and leverage. A firmware sensitivity curve changes how movement is translated into output. One does not substitute for the other. Cycle ratings, where published, describe specified component tests—not a guaranteed number of years of gaming.

04 / ELECTRICAL COMPATIBILITY

Five pins aren’t a speed rating.

First ask what is being counted: pins on one sensor, contacts on a connector, or all terminals on a mechanism. “3-pin” and “5-pin” are not universal interchangeability standards.

3-PIN / COMMON ANALOG ARRANGEMENT

More than one sensor technology

A conventional axis circuit can use three connections. Compatible magnetic replacements can occupy this kind of interface too. Three pins do not necessarily mean a carbon-track potentiometer.

5-PIN / SPECIFIC IMPLEMENTATION

Requires a matching design

Additional connections may support a different interface, but their functions must come from the design documentation. You cannot infer the protocol, resolution or polling rate from the count.

Concrete example: Marius / MIDAS.

Battle Beaver documents its MIDAS option as a five-pin Hall-effect system. In its documented JDM-5X configurations, the three-pin and five-pin boards and mechanisms are not interchangeable; changing system requires the corresponding PCB. This is a compatibility rule for that implementation, not a rule for every five-pin product. Builder’s technical documentation ↗

For any proposed swap, confirm footprint, pin functions, supply voltage, output levels, axis orientation, supported firmware and calibration. A part fitting the holes is only one check. Never assume two additional pins automatically make a stick faster or more accurate.

05 / CONTROLLER BOARDS

The board matters. So does its revision.

A mainboard runs the controller. A remap board adds ways to activate existing controls. A calibration daughterboard adjusts a compatible stick circuit. A Windows USB filter changes host-side behavior. These are different jobs.

Marius / MH family

Maker + builder documentation

Marius replacement boards are a separate hardware platform, not an original Sony board with an overclock setting. The maker’s October 2025 MH4 Analog announcement describes a PC-only DS4-format board, supplied without sticks, without rumble, sound or gyro. It says analog and digital variants have approximately the same speed. Those details belong to that product generation. Maker announcement ↗

MH4, MH5 and other family/build names need their own specifications. Battle Beaver currently lists DS4-, DualSense- and Xbox-format Marius builds with native 8K PC polling; a shell format does not establish support for its original console. Builder’s current family listings ↗

The developer documentation distinguishes configuration mode from normal operation: its setup mode runs at 100–300 Hz. Measuring in that mode does not establish gaming-mode performance. DeepPoll measures polling, not the full input-to-screen delay. Developer docs ↗ DeepPoll release notes ↗

FireBird / Firebird A*

Revision-specific documentation

The creator’s older, multi-form-factor FireBird manual explicitly covers PCB v2.4, tool v2.15 and firmware v2.24. It documents 1,000 Hz in non-Switch modes. This is not a verified specification for every DS4 or A* build. Newer generations must not inherit every spec from that manual—or have their specs applied backward to it. Creator’s scoped manual ↗

The creator separates legacy firmware tools from the newer browser configurator. Confirm the board, firmware branch, input mode and tool together. The configurator also warns about configuration loss when downgrading. A brand name or a screenshot of a settings menu is not enough to establish a build’s performance. Creator’s configurator ↗

Helium Strike / HyperStrike listings

Verification limited

These names appear in custom-builder listings, but a complete current hardware specification and revision cross-reference could not be verified from the HS2 configuration page during this research. We therefore do not assign a universal polling rate, console list or retained-feature list to the name. HS2 configuration page ↗

Ask the builder for the exact PCB and firmware, whether it is a legacy or current product, and the supported mode on your platform. An inaccessible configurator is a research limit, not evidence that a controller is faulty.

SuiOvOi

Configurator evidence

Its configuration interface exposes USB report rate, stick sampling rate, output precision and sampling precision as separate controls. That is a useful illustration of why those figures are not interchangeable. Available settings do not prove effective sensor accuracy, measured latency or compatibility of every board sold under the name. Configuration interface ↗

GP2040-CE

Open project documentation

GP2040-CE is controller firmware for RP2040-based boards, widely used in custom arcade and leverless controllers. It is not one universal drop-in DualSense motherboard. Its console documentation makes authentication and game-category requirements explicit. “PS5 mode” does not mean unrestricted support for every PS5 game. Official compatibility documentation ↗

Remap kits: eXtremeRate and similar

Exact-SKU documentation

For example, eXtremeRate’s RISE4 Plus MAX SKU HKCPFJ001M3 adds rear controls and lists particular DualSense BDM revisions. That is a remap kit, not a replacement input-processing mainboard. Compatibility and soldering requirements belong to the specific kit and functions selected. Manufacturer’s kit specification ↗

8K does not inherently remove features.

Audio, rumble, gyro, touch tracking, battery use and wireless support depend on the board and complete build. A physical touchpad button can remain even when touch tracking does not. Request a retained-feature list for the exact product.

06 / READING THE NUMBERS

Reports per second ≠ input lag.

USB polling interval describes the host’s service schedule. Report rate counts reports actually delivered; it need not match the requested polling rate. Sampling rate describes how often inputs are acquired internally. Latency is time between a defined action and a defined observation. A claim needs to say which one was measured. USB interval documentation ↗ DeepPoll’s measurement scope ↗

An 8K interval does not establish 0.125 ms button latency, fresh stick data in every report, or a 0.125 ms input-to-screen response. Input scanning, processing, transport, game timing and display timing still sit in the path.

What HIDUSBF can—and cannot—change

The hidusbf filter can alter USB polling behavior for supported configurations on Windows. It does not replace the sensor, increase the physical ADC’s resolution or rewrite the controller’s internal input loop. Requesting a rate does not prove the device produces fresh reports at that rate. Native high-rate firmware and host-side overclocking are different implementations. Original driver documentation ↗

A Windows USB filter does not overclock Bluetooth, and it does not alter a console’s USB stack. Higher rates can also add processing overhead; verify stability and the result on the actual host.

Bits are not the same thing as usable precision

An 8-bit field has 256 possible codes; 12 bits has 4,096; 16 bits has 65,536. That says how many values can be represented. Noise, sensor quality, conversion, filtering and mechanical repeatability determine how much of that is useful.

XInput uses signed 16-bit stick fields, but an API field does not prove the controller captured 16 bits of independent physical position information. Upscaling or interpolation cannot create missing measurements. Microsoft’s XINPUT_GAMEPAD structure ↗

Read tests with their method attached

A useful latency result states the exact controller, board and firmware, connection, input mode, button or stick tested, test method and distribution of results. A best-case screenshot without those details cannot support a universal “fastest controller” claim. GP2040-CE publishes a defined method and mode-specific results rather than equating its polling interval with measured latency. Project performance documentation ↗

A browser gamepad visualizer is useful for checking input behavior. If its loop runs with requestAnimationFrame, observation normally follows display refresh. Do not treat that page’s visual refresh as a direct USB bus measurement. Browser timing documentation ↗

07 / CALIBRATION & TESTING

A clean circle isn’t the whole test.

Center calibration
Teaches the supported system where neutral is. It can correct an offset; it cannot repair a loose mechanism or damaged sensor.
Range calibration
Maps the reachable extremes. Check every direction and diagonal after a change, not just full left and right.
Inner deadzone
Ignores movement near center. It can hide small offsets or noise, at the cost of a region with no response. Zero deadzone is not automatically the best usable setting.
Outer deadzone
Determines where full output is reached. An aggressive setting can clip travel and make reaching maximum output easier without improving the sensor.
Response curve
Changes the relationship between physical displacement and output. Linear, aggressive or softened response is a preference and game interaction, not a sensor type.
Filtering & snapback
Filtering can suppress noise or the brief opposite input after a released stick crosses center. Its effect on responsiveness depends on the algorithm and setting.

Configuration concepts: FireBird creator’s manual and Microsoft’s deadzone examples.

A low circularity-error score only describes the output under that test and mapping. Firmware can reshape the outer boundary; a perfect-looking circle does not establish linear small movements, good center return, low latency or mechanical quality. Test those separately.

After a repair or stick replacement

Follow the exact module and board’s procedure. GuliKit, for example, instructs users to fit the case and screws before calibration and lists controller-specific tools. Do not assume a generic Windows or browser calibration command writes the right settings to every controller. Maker’s calibration instructions ↗

For an OEM PlayStation controller, use tools that explicitly support its model and revision. A replacement board emulating a DualShock is not necessarily compatible with Sony calibration commands. The original dualshock-tools project documents its own scope. Upstream project ↗

A practical check

  1. Observe untouched sticks for offset and jitter.
  2. Operate the triggers with both sticks untouched; look for unintended stick movement.
  3. Make tiny movements; check for dead spots and jumps.
  4. Sweep slowly through the full range, including diagonals.
  5. Release from different directions and check return to center and snapback.
  6. Check at your real in-game deadzone and curve, then repeat on the connection you use.

These checks assess behavior. They do not independently measure input latency or certify future durability.

08 / COMMON MISCONCEPTIONS

Claims worth questioning.

Open a claim for the short correction. The supporting detail and sources are in the sections above.

“TMR is another name for Hall effect.”

Both sense magnetic fields, but use different physical effects. Neither term specifies the entire stick or controller. Read about the sensors →

“A magnetic stick can never drift.”

Contactless sensing removes a source of wear. It does not stop mechanical wear, calibration errors or other failures. “Drift-resistant” is a more useful description than a lifetime guarantee. What anti-drift means →

“JS13 and TMR are competing technologies.”

JS13 is a model-family designation; JS13 Pro+ is offered as TMR. Specify the complete part and controller variant. Module names explained →

“Five-pin means TMR, faster polling or universal fit.”

Pin count establishes none of those things. The documented MIDAS five-pin example uses Hall sensing and its matching Marius board. Check the interface →

“8,000 Hz means 0.125 ms input lag.”

0.125 ms is the nominal report interval. Latency requires a measurement with defined endpoints. Buttons and sticks may also have different processing paths. Read the numbers →

“A 16-bit output guarantees 16-bit physical accuracy.”

Output format, sensor resolution and effective precision are different. Filling a larger field cannot recover input detail that was never measured. Bits and usable precision →

“A PS5 shell or DS4 mode proves console support.”

A replacement board has its own protocol and authentication requirements. Check the exact console and game category, including any adapter requirement. Board compatibility →

“Calibration repairs worn hardware.”

Calibration changes how readings are interpreted. It cannot replace damaged contacts, worn plastic or a weak spring, and a large deadzone can conceal a problem. Calibration limits →

09 / BEFORE YOU BUY

Ask for the complete specification.

A useful product listing should let you answer these questions without guessing from a shell photo or a headline number.

  1. What exactly is installed? Controller format, PCB model/revision, firmware, full stick model, sensor technology and electrical interface.
  2. Where does it work? Your platform and game, wired/wireless mode, native support or adapter requirement, and any authentication or feature limits.
  3. What stays and what goes? Audio, gyro, rumble, adaptive or analog triggers, touch tracking, touchpad button, battery and wireless operation.
  4. What was actually measured? Polling versus latency, button versus stick results, conditions, firmware and complete test method.
  5. How is it maintained? Correct calibration tool, update/recovery instructions, replacement availability and the seller’s written repair/warranty terms.

The sensible choice is the one whose compatibility, physical feel and measured behavior suit your use. A newer sensor can be an improvement without making every older controller obsolete.
